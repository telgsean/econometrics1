Exercise 1

(B.26 in ME) Let \(Q\) be a random variable with \[ E(Q) = (n-k)\sigma^{2}, \qquad \Var(Q) = 2(n-k)\sigma^{4}, \] where \(0 < k < n\). Now define an estimator \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{m} = Q/m\) of \(\sigma^{2}\).

  1. Compute the mean and variance of \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n-k}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}\).
  2. Compute the mean squared error of \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n-k}\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{n}\).
  3. Determine \(m\) such that the estimator \(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{m}\) has the lowest mean squared error.
CautionSolution

  1. The mean and variance of \(Q/m\) are \[ E(Q/m)= E(Q)/m = (n-k) \sigma^2/m \] and \[ \Var(Q/m) = \Var(Q)/m^2 = 2(n-k)\sigma^4/m^2. \] This implies \[ \begin{aligned} &E(\hat\sigma_{n-k}^2) =(n-k)\sigma^2/(n-k) =\sigma^2, \\ &E(\hat\sigma_{n}^2) =(n-k)\sigma^2/n <\sigma^2, \\ &\Var(\hat\sigma_{n-k}^2) =2(n-k)\sigma^4/(n-k)^2 =2\sigma^4/(n-k), \\ &\Var(\hat\sigma_n^2) =2(n-k)\sigma^4/n^2. \end{aligned} \] The first estimator is unbiased, while the second estimator is biased and underestimates \(\sigma^2\). But the first estimator has a larger variance than the second estimator, because \(2/(n-k) > 2(n-k)/n^2\).

  2. The bias of \(Q/m\) as an estimator of \(\sigma^2\) is \[ \text{bias}(Q/m)= E(Q/m) - \sigma^2=(n-k-m)\sigma^2/m, \] and the mean squared error is \[ \begin{aligned} \text{MSE}(Q/m)&= \Var(Q/m) +\text{bias}^2(Q/m) \\ &= [2(n-k)+(n-k-m)^2]\sigma^4/m^2. \end{aligned} \] Based on the general formula for the mean squared error, we find \[ \text{MSE}(\hat\sigma_{n-k}^{2}) = 2\sigma^4/(n-k) \] and \[ \text{MSE}(\hat\sigma_{n}^{2}) = (2(n-k)+k^2)\sigma^4/n^2, \] from which we see that \[ \text{MSE}(\hat\sigma_{n-k}^{2})<\text{MSE}(\hat\sigma_{n}^{2}) \iff k>4 \text{ and } n>\frac{k(k-2)}{k-4}, \] which may or may not be the case depending on the values of \(n\) and \(k\).

  3. Since \[ \text{MSE}(\hat\sigma_{m}^{2}) = \frac{[2(n-k)+(n-k-m)^2]\sigma^4}{m^2}, \] we find the optimal value of \(m\) by minimizing \[ f(m)=\frac{2(n-k)+(n-k-m)^2}{m^2}. \] Differentiating \(f\) gives \[ \begin{aligned} f'(m) &=\frac{-2(n-k-m)m^2 - 4(n-k)m - 2(n-k-m)^2 m}{m^4}\\ &=\frac{-2(n-k-m+2)(n-k)}{m^3}, \end{aligned} \] and this is zero if and only if \(m=n-k+2\). Hence, \(\hat\sigma_{n-k+2}^2\), although biased, has a smaller mean squared error than the unbiased estimator \(\hat\sigma_{n-k}^2\).

Exercise 2

(1.12, 1.13 and 1.14 in ME) Suppose we minimize \[ \phi(\beta) = (y - X\beta)'(y - X\beta) + \lambda \beta'\beta, \] where scalar \(\lambda > 0\). This is called ridge regression.

  1. Show that \(b_{\text{ridge}} = (X'X + \lambda I_{k})^{-1} X'y\) is the solution to the minimization problem.

  2. What could be the motivation to use ridge regression? Hint: what problem can occur in least squares but not in ridge? Compare \(b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\) and \(b_{\text{ridge}} = (X'X + \lambda I_{k})^{-1} X'y\).

  3. Show that the ridge solution attains a minimum.

  4. Suppose we are given the design matrix: \[ X = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & \phantom{-}1 \\ \phantom{-}1 & -1 \\ \phantom{-}1 & \phantom{-}1 \end{pmatrix}. \] Express \(b_{\text{ridge}} = (X'X + \lambda I_{k})^{-1} X'y\) in terms of \(b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y\).

This exercise is a bit more difficult (old exam question). Therefore, you are advised to first go through 1.3 and 1.5 in ME. For b., consider an easy example of a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(X'X\) that is singular.

CautionHint

  1. Note that there is only one extra term compared to least squares. Appendix A shows how to take a derivative with respect to a quadratic term.

  2. Suppose one of the assumptions does not hold. What potential problem of \(X'X\) can \(X'X + \lambda I_{k}\) circumvent?

  3. The second derivative can formally show that the ridge solution attains a minimum.

  4. \(b_{\text{ridge}}\) is a rescaled version of \(b\).

Exercise 3

(1.6 in ME) Prove that \(R^{2}\) never decreases when including an additional regressor in the model. Hint: Consider the sum of squared residuals of the two models.

CautionShort solution
  • For the model \(y = X\beta + u\), the coefficient of determination is given by \(R^{2} = 1 - \frac{e'e}{y'Ay}\).
  • Consider a model that has one additional regressor, i.e. \(X_{*} = (X : x_{k+1})\). All quantities regarding this new model have an asterisk.
  • Let \(u\) denote the error term of the original model using design matrix \(X\) and \(u_{*}\) the error term of the extended model using design matrix \(X_{*}\).
  • The key part to see is that \[ e_{*}'e_{*} = \text{min}_{\beta_{*}} \ u_{*}'u_{*} \leq \text{min}_{\beta_{*} \text{ with } \beta_{k+1} = 0} \ u_{*}'u_{*} = \text{min}_{\beta} \ u'u = e'e. \]
  • Since \(y'Ay\) is the same in both models, we get that \(R^{2}_{*} \geq R^{2}\).

Exercise 4

(2.14 in ME) Suppose we are given the linear regression model \[ y = X\beta + u, \quad E(u) = 0, \quad \Var(u) = \Omega, \] with \(X\) a nonrandom \(n \times k\) matrix of full column rank, \(\beta\) a \(k \times 1\) coefficient vector, and \(\Omega\) a positive definite \(n \times n\) variance matrix, which may be nondiagonal.

  1. Consider the linear unbiased estimator \(A'y\) of the linear combination \(W'\beta\) for some given matrix \(W\). Show that \(A'X = W'\).

  2. For the case \(\Omega = \sigma^{2}I_{n}\), find the choice of \(A\) that minimizes \(\text{var}(A'y)\). Use the decomposition \(D = A - X(X'X)^{-1}W\) in your solution.

  3. Apply a transformation to the linear regression model to derive the best linear unbiased estimator in the case where \(\Omega\) is a known positive definite matrix.

This is a slight extension to the proof of the Gauss-Markov theorem in Section 2.10 of ITE. Try to understand the proving strategy: we will also use it in the context of prediction.

CautionSolution

  1. Unbiasedness requires \[ E(A'y) = A'E(y) = A'X\beta = W'\beta. \] Since this has to hold for all \(\beta\), we conclude \(A'X = W'\).

  2. Since \(A'X = W'\), we see that \[ D'X = A'X - W'(X'X)^{-1}X'X - A'X - W' = 0. \] This result is useful for the following computation (since it is a decomposition, the crossterms disappear) as \[ \begin{aligned} A'A &= (D + X(X'X)^{-1}W)'(D + X(X'X)^{-1}W) \\ &= D'D + W'(X'X)^{-1}X'X(X'X)^{-1}W \\ &= D'D + W'(X'X)^{1}W. \end{aligned} \] Since \(\Var(A'y) = A'\sigma^{2}I_{n}A = \sigma^{2}A'A\), this is minimized when \(D'D = 0\), that is when \(D = 0\), so that $A = X(X’X)^{-1}W.

  3. Since \(\Omega\) is positive definite, its unique square root is defined and we can write \(\Omega = \Omega^{1/2}\Omega^{1/2}\). This square root is also positive definite, hence nonsingular. If we now transform \[ y_{*} = \Omega^{-1/2}y, \quad X_{*} = \Omega^{-1/2}X, \quad u_{*} = \Omega^{-1/2}u, \] then \(y_{*} = X_{*}\beta + u_{*}\) is the classical linear regression model where \(E(u_{*}) =0\) and \(\Var(u_{*}) = I_{n}\). Thus, the LS estimator for \(\beta\) is given by \[ \hat{\beta} = (X_{*}'X_{*})^{-1}X_{*}'y_{*} = (X'\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1} X'\Omega^{-1}y, \] and is the best linear unbiased estimator of \(\beta\). This procedure is known as generalized least squares (GLS), if \(\Omega\) needs to be estimated beforehand it is called feasible GLS. It is a generalization of weighted least squares, which allows for heteroskedasticity. GLS also allows for nonzero autocorrelation (i.e. correlation between the elements in the vector \(u\)).

Exercise 6

(2.8 in ME) A researcher wants to estimate the following model \[ y = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{1}^{2} + u, \] because she believes that \(x_{1}\) does not have a constant marginal effect on \(y\) but rather a diminishing marginal effect.

  1. Does the proposed model violate the linearity assumption?

  2. An advantage of the multiple linear regression model is that it facilitates ceteris paribus analysis. Why is this difficult here?

  3. In case of diminishing marginal effects, what would be the signs of \(\beta_{1}\) and \(\beta_{2}\)?

  4. Determine how a change in \(x_{1}\) affects \(y\). At which value of \(x_{1}\) does the function take its maximum?

  5. Given an example of an empirical application where this modeling technique might be appropriate.

CautionSolution

  1. The model has to be linear in the \(\beta\) parameters, not in \(X\). Thus, the linearity assumption is not violated.

  2. The multiple linear regression model is often used to study the effect of a change in one of the regressors, say \(x_{1}\), on \(y\), while controlling for the effects of other variables that influence \(y\). Suppose for the moment that the model does not contain \(x_{1}^{2}\), but instead another regressor, say \(x_{2}\). Then we could study the effect of \(x_{1}\) on \(y\), while keeping \(x_{2}\) fixed. However, in the case at hand, both \(x_{1}\) and \(x_{1}^{2}\) appear in the model, and therefore we can no longer interpret \(\beta_{1}\) as the ceteris paribus effect of \(x_{1}\) on \(y\), since a change in \(x_{1}\) also affects \(x_{1}^{2}\).

  3. By choosing \(\beta_{1} > 0\) and \(\beta_{2} < 0\), we obtain a parabola with inverted \(U\)-shape when we plot \(y\) against \(x_{1}\). A change in \(x_{1}\) close to the maximum of the function provides a smaller increase in \(y\) than a change in \(x_1\) further away from the maximum, which is the essence of diminishing marginal effects.

  4. Define \(g(x_{1}) = \beta_{0} + \beta_{1}x_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{1}^{2}\), then the effect of a change in \(x_{1}\) on \(y\) is given by \[ \frac{d g(x_{1})}{d x_{1}} = \beta_{1} + 2\beta_{2}x_{1}. \] Setting this derivative equal to zero, leads to \(x_{1}^{*} = -\beta_{1}/(2\beta_{2})\) as the value of \(x_{1}\) for which the function \(g(x_{1})\) takes its maximum.

  5. A well-known example is the relationship between experience and salary. An additional year of experience is typically more valuable for a relatively inexperienced worker than for a worker who has held the position for many years.

Exercise 7

(2.15 in ME). Consider the standard linear regression model \(y = X\beta + u\), where \(y\) and \(u\) are of order \(n \times 1\), \(\beta\) is of order \(k \times 1\), and \(X\) is an \(n \times k\) matrix with full column rank. We assume that \(E(u) = 0\) and \(\Var(u) = \sigma^{2} I_{n}\).

  1. Show that the LS residuals \(e\) are equal to \(My\) and \(Mu\).

  2. Compute the mean and variance of the residuals \(e\).

  3. Show that the variance matrix of \(e\) cannot have full rank and explain why.

CautionHint

  1. Showing that \(MX = 0\) may be helpful.

  2. Use the properties of the matrix \(M\).

  3. Use the trace operator to calculate the rank of \(M\).

Exercise 8

(2.17 in ME) Let us split the data into an “in-sample” data set \((y_{1},X_{1})\) and an “out-of-sample” data set \((y_{2},X_{2})\), where the in-sample set is based on \(n\) observations and the out-of-sample set is based on \(m\) observations, so that the dimensions are \(y_{1}\) \((n \times 1)\), \(X_{1}\) \((n \times k)\), \(y_{2}\) \((m \times 1)\), and \(X_{2}\) \((m \times k)\). We assume \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\) are generated by \[ y_{1} = X_{1}\beta + u_{1}, \qquad y_{2} = X_{2}\beta + u_{2}, \] where \(E(u_{1}) = 0\), \(E(u_{2}) = 0\), \(\Var(u_{1}) = \sigma^{2}I_{n}\), \(\Var(u_{2}) = \sigma^{2}I_{m}\), and \(\text{Cov}(u_{1},u_{2}) = 0\). Assume that the standard assumptions of the linear model hold.

Show that the predictor \(X_{2}\hat{\beta}\) with \(\hat{\beta} = (X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}'y_{1}\) is best linear unbiased. That is, among all predictors of the form \(\hat{y}_{2} = Ly_{1}\) with the property \(E(y_{2} - \hat{y}_{2}) = 0\), show that \(L_{*} = X_{2}(X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}'\) is the optimal choice in the sense that \(L_{*}y_{1}\) minimizes the variance of the prediction error \(y_{2} - \hat{y}_{2}\).

CautionHint

This exercise requires a very similar proving style as we used for finding the best linear unbiased estimator. You can solve it by following the steps below:

That is,

  • Show that the proposed predictor is indeed linear.

  • Show that it has an expected prediction error of zero (this makes the predictor “unbiased”).

  • Compute the variance of the prediction error.

  • Afterwards, do the same for the “other” predictor, which involves the matrix \(D = L_{*} - L\).

  • Show that to be the “best” estimator, i.e. in terms of smallest variance possible, \(D=0\), giving the variance that you computed for the proposed predictor.

Exercise 1

(2.18 in ME) Let \(y = X_{1}\beta_{1} + X_{2}\beta_{2} + u\). Let \(\widehat{\beta}_{1r}\) be the estimator of \(\beta_{1}\) by regressing \(y\) on \(X_{1}\) alone. Let \(\widehat{\beta}_{1u}\) and \(\widehat{\beta}_{2u}\) be the estimators of \(\beta_{1}\) and \(\beta_{2}\) when \(y\) is regressed on both \(X_{1}\) and \(X_{2}\). Show that \(\Var(\widehat{\beta}_{1r}) \leq \Var(\widehat{\beta}_{1u})\). What is the implication of this result? You may use the fact that \(\Cov(\widehat{\beta}_{2u}, \widehat{\beta}_{1r}) = 0\).

CautionSolution

The full solution can be found in the book Mastering Econometrics, Exercise 2.18.

Exercise 2

(2.19 in ME) Suppose that the DGP is given by \[ y = X_{1}\beta_{1} + X_{2}\beta_{2} + u, \qquad u \sim N(0, \sigma^{2}_{u}I_{n}), \] which we will refer to as the unrestricted model. We denote the least-squares estimator of \(\beta_{1}\) in this model by \(\widehat{\beta}_{1u}\). We also consider the restricted model \[ y = X_{1}\beta_{1} + u_{1}, \] and we denote the least-squares estimator of \(\beta_{1}\) in this model as \(\widehat{\beta}_{1r}\). It can be shown that \[ \text{bias}(\widehat{\beta}_{1r}) = (X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}'X_{2}\beta_{2}, \] and \[ \Var(\widehat{\beta}_{1u}) - \Var(\widehat{\beta}_{1r}) = \sigma^{2}_{u}\Delta, \] where \[ \Delta = (X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}'X_{2}(X_{2}'M_{1}X_{2})^{-1}X_{2}'X_{1}(X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}, \] and \(M_{1} = I_{n} - X_{1}(X_{1}'X_{1})^{-1}X_{1}'\).

CautionSolution

The full solution can be found in the book Mastering Econometrics, Exercise 2.19.

Exercise 3

(3.8 in ME) Production \(Q\) is determined by a Cobb-Douglas production function, which we estimate in logarithmic form as \[ \log Q_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{k}\log K_i + \beta_{l}\log L_i + u_i. \] This yields the following result: \[ \widehat{\log Q} = 1.37 + \underset{(0.257)}{0.632}\log K + \underset{(0.219)}{0.452} \log L, \] where the standard errors are in parentheses. We further have \(R^2 = 0.98\) and \(\Cov(\widehat\beta_{k}, \widehat\beta_{l}) = 0.055\).

  1. Test the hypothesis that the output elasticities with respect to capital \(K\) and labor \(L\) are the same.
  2. Test the hypothesis that there are constant returns to scale.
CautionShort solution
  1. We find \(t = 2.85\), which is the realization of a \(t(n-3)\) distribution. We do not know the value of \(n\). If \(n\leq6\) then we do not reject the null hypothesis at the \(5\%\) significance level, but if \(n>6\) we do.
  2. We find \(t = 0.18\), which is too small to be significant at any sample size, and we can therefore not reject the hypothesis of constant returns to scale.

Exercise 4

(3.9 in ME) Consider the linear regression model \[ y = X\beta + u, \] where \(X\) is an \(n \times k\) matrix and \(\beta = (\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})'\). Show that the \(F\)-statistic for a single restriction \(\beta_{j} = 0\) is equal to the square of the \(t\)-statistic to test \(\beta_{j} = 0\).

CautionSolution

The full solution can be found in the book Mastering Econometrics, Exercise 3.9.

Exercise 5

Show that the test statistic \[ \frac{w'\widehat{\beta} - r}{\sqrt{s^{2}w'(X'X)^{-1}w}} \sim t(n-k) \] reduces to \[ \frac{\widehat{\beta}_{j}}{\sqrt{s^{2}v_{jj}}} \sim t(n-k) \] for the restriction \(\beta_{j} = 0\). Note that \(V = (X'X)^{-1}\).

CautionSolution

The restriction \(\beta_{j} = 0\) can be represented in the form \(w'\beta = r\) by choosing \(w = e_{j}\), where \(e_{j}\) is the \(k \times 1\) basis vector where the \(j\)-th entry is one and all other entries are zero, and \(r = 0\). Thus, in the numerator, we obtain \(w'\widehat{\beta} = e_{j}'\widehat{\beta} = \widehat{\beta}_{j}\).

Regarding the denominator, we note that \(w'(X'X)^{-1}w\) can be written as \(e_{j}'Ve_{j}\) in this case. We have \[ Ve_{j} = \begin{pmatrix} v_{1j} \\ v_{2j} \\ \vdots \\ v_{kj} \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{ and } e_{j}'Ve_{j} = v_{jj}. \]

This gives the desired result.

Exercise 6

(3.10, 3.11, 3.12 in ME) Consider the model \(y = X\beta + u\). We impose the restriction \(R\beta=r\), where \(R\) has full row rank. The least-squares (LS) estimator of \(\beta\) in the restricted model is obtained by minimizing \((y-X\beta)'(y-X\beta)\) under the restriction \(R\beta=r\).

  1. Show that the restricted LS estimator is given by \[ \tilde\beta = \hat\beta - (X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r), \] where \(\hat\beta\) is the estimator in the unrestricted model.

  2. Show that \[ \tilde{u}=\hat{u} + X(X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r), \] where \(\hat{u}\) denotes the residual vector from the unrestricted model and \(\tilde{u}\) the residual vector from the restricted model.

  3. The \(F\)-statistic for testing the hypothesis \(R\beta=r\) is given by \[ F = \frac{(R\hat\beta - r)'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r)/m}{\hat{u}'\hat{u}/(n-k)} \sim F(m,n-k), \] where \(\hat{u}\) is the residual vector from the unrestricted regression. Show that \(F\) can also be written as \[ F = \frac{(\tilde{u}'\tilde{u} - \hat{u}'\hat{u})/m}{\hat{u}'\hat{u}/(n-k)}, \] where \(\tilde{u}\) denotes the residual vector from the restricted regression.

CautionSolution

  1. The objective function is \(\phi(\beta)=(y-X\beta)'(y-X\beta)\) and the constraint is \(R\beta-r=0\). Since we are essentially differentiating a square, it is common practice to minimize \(\phi(\beta)/2\), but this is for aesthetic convenience only. The Lagrangian is then \[ \psi(\beta)=(y-X\beta)'(y-X\beta)/2 -l'(R\beta-r), \] where \(l\) is a vector of Lagrangian multipliers. Taking the derivative with respect to \(\beta\) gives \[ \frac{\partial\psi(\beta)}{\partial\beta'} = -(y-X\beta)'X -l'R, \] so that the first-order conditions are \[ X'(y-X\beta) + R'l=0, \qquad R\beta=r. \] To solve these two equations in the two unknowns \(\beta\) and \(l\), we premultiply the first equation by \(R(X'X)^{-1}\), giving \[ R(X'X)^{-1}R'l = - R(X'X)^{-1}X'(y-X\beta) = - (R\hat\beta - r), \] where \(\hat\beta=(X'X)^{-1}X'y\) is the estimator in the unrestricted model. Since the matrix \(R(X'X)^{-1}R'\) is nonsingular, we can solve \(l\) as \[ l = - (R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r), \] so that \[ X'(y-X\beta) + R'l = X'y-X'X\beta - R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r) =0, \] from which we solve the restricted estimator as \[ \tilde\beta = \hat\beta - (X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \] To show that the matrix \(V=R(X'X)^{-1}R'\) is nonsingular, we argue as follows. Let \(A=(X'X)^{-1/2}R'\). Then, \(V=A'A\) and \(r(V)=r(A'A)=r(A)=r(R')=m\), where we used the facts that \(R\) has full row rank and that \(r(A'A)=r(A)\) for any matrix \(A\); see also Exercise A.15 in Mastering Econometrics. This shows that \(V\) is an \(m \times m\) matrix of rank \(m\), hence nonsingular.

  2. We find \[ \tilde{\beta} = \hat\beta - (X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \] Premultiplying this expression by \(X\) yields \[ X\tilde\beta = X\hat\beta - X(X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \] Since \(\hat{u}=y-X\hat\beta\) and \(\tilde{u}=y-X\tilde\beta\), we then find \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{u} &= y-X\tilde\beta \\ &= y-X\hat\beta + X(X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r) \\ &= \hat{u} + X(X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \end{aligned} \]

  3. We know that \[ \tilde{u} = \hat{u} + X(X'X)^{-1}R'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \] In fact, this relationship is a decomposition, because the two vectors on the right-hand side are orthogonal to each other, which follows from the fact that \(X'\hat{u}=0\). Then, letting \(V=R(X'X)^{-1}R'\), \[ \begin{aligned} \tilde{u}'\tilde{u} &= \hat{u}'\hat{u} + (R\hat\beta - r)'V^{-1}R(X'X)^{-1}X'X (X'X)^{-1}R'V^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r)\\ &=\hat{u}'\hat{u} + (R\hat\beta - r)'V^{-1}VV^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r)\\ &=\hat{u}'\hat{u} + (R\hat\beta - r)'V^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r). \end{aligned} \] It follows that \[ \frac{(R\hat\beta - r)'(R(X'X)^{-1}R')^{-1}(R\hat\beta - r)/m}{\hat{u}'\hat{u}/(n-k)} = \frac{(\tilde{u}'\tilde{u} - \hat{u}'\hat{u})/m}{\hat{u}'\hat{u}/(n-k)}. \]

Exercise 7

(3.18 in ME) Consider a sample of size \(n=12\) from the linear regression model \(y = X\beta + u\), where \(X = (\imath : x_{2} : x_{3})\). We know that \(\bar{x}_2=\bar{x}_3=0\), \(\bar{y}=2.5\), and \[ \begin{aligned} x_{2}'x_{2}^{} &= 300, \qquad & x_{2}'x_{3}^{} &= -205, \qquad & x_{3}'x_{3}^{} &= 182,\\ x_{2}'y &= 3, \qquad & x_{3}'y &= -12, \qquad & y'y &= 470. \end{aligned} \]

  1. Find the least-squares (LS) estimate of \(\beta\).
  2. Calculate \(s^{2}\), \(R^2\), and \(\bar{R}^2\).
  3. Comment on the values of \(R^2\) and \(\bar{R}^2\). Are they plausible?

(Similar exercises in ME: 3.1, 3.14, 3.19)

CautionSolution

  1. The matrix \(X'X\) is given by \[ X'X = \begin{pmatrix} n & \imath'x_{2} & \imath'x_{3} \\ \imath'x_{2} & x_{2}'x_{2}^{} & x_{2}'x_{3}^{} \\ \imath'x_{3} & x_{3}'x_{2}^{} & x_{3}'x_{3}^{} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 & \phantom{-}0 & \phantom{-}0 \\ 0 & \phantom{-}300 & -205 \\ 0 & -205 & \phantom{-}182 \end{pmatrix}. \] This matrix is block-diagonal, and we write \[ X'X = \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 0' \\ 0 & Z \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z= \begin{pmatrix} \phantom{-}300 & -205 \\ -205 & \phantom{-}182 \end{pmatrix}. \] The determinant of \(Z\) is \(|Z|=300 \times 182 - (-205)^{2} = 12575\), which is nonzero, so that \(Z\) and hence \(X'X\) are nonsingular. Thus, \[ (X'X)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/12 & 0' \\ 0 & Z^{-1} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z^{-1}= \frac{1}{12575} \begin{pmatrix} 182 & 205 \\ 205 & 300 \end{pmatrix}, \] and \[ X'y = \begin{pmatrix} \imath'y \\ x_{2}'y \\ x_{3}'y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \phantom{-}30 \\ \phantom{-}3 \\ -12 \end{pmatrix}. \] Consequently, we obtain the LS estimates \[ \hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'y = \begin{pmatrix} 5/2 \\ -1914/12575 \\ -2985/12575 \end{pmatrix}. \]

  2. Let \(A=I_n-\imath\imath'/n\). Since \[ \begin{aligned} e'e &= y'y - \hat{\beta}'X'y = 470 - 77.392 = 392.608, \\ y'Ay &= y'y -n\,\bar{y}^2 = 470 -12\times(2.5)^2 = 395, \end{aligned} \] we find \[ \begin{aligned} s^2 &= e'e/(n-k)= 392.608 / 9 = 43.623, \\ R^2 &= 1- e'e/y'Ay =1 - 392.608 / 395 =0.006, \\ \bar{R}^2 &= 1-\frac{s^2}{y'Ay/(n-1)} = 1 - \frac{43.623}{395/11} = -0.215. \end{aligned} \]

  3. Since we have a model with intercept, we know that \(R^2 \in [0,1]\), and this is indeed the case here. The fact that \(R^2\) is close to zero means that the fit is not good. We also know that \(\bar{R}^2\) is not restricted to the \([0,1]\) interval and, when the fit is not so good, it can become negative - as it does here.

Week 4 - Exercises

Author

Sean Telg

Revisiting previous topics

This week we start with some relatively short exercises on material covering the first three weeks. Because we are halfway the first part of the course, it is a good moment to check whether you can comfortably answer questions about this material.

Exercise 1

For the simple linear regression model, show that \[ \widehat{\beta}_{2} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}y_{i} - \overline{x}\overline{y}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2} - \overline{x}^{2}} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}}. \]

CautionShort solution

This solution only shows the numerator, the denominator is done analogously.

Note that \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}y_{i} - \overline{x}\overline{y} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}y_{i} - \overline{x}\overline{y} - \overline{x}\overline{y} + \overline{x}\overline{y} \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}y_{i} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}\overline{y} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_{i} \overline{x} + \overline{x}\overline{y} \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i}y_{i} - x_{i}\overline{y} - y_{i}\overline{x} - \overline{x}\overline{y}) \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i}(y_{i} - \overline{y}) - \overline{x}(y_{i} - \overline{y})) \\ &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y}) \end{aligned} \] In the first equality, we make use of telescoping. In the second and third equality, we ensure that the first two terms are both expressed in terms of \(x_{i}\) and the last two terms in terms of \(\overline{x}\). This is convenient for collecting terms, which we do in the last two equalities.

Exercise 2

In the linear regression model with intercept, show that \(\overline{y} = \overline{\widehat{y}}\).

CautionSolution

Note that \(y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i} = e_{i}\). Taking sums and multiplying by \(\frac{1}{n}\) on both sides yields \[ \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} y_{i} - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat{y}_{i} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_{i}. \] We note that \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} e_{i} = 0\) by the first-order condition of least squares. Thus, \(\overline{y} = \overline{\widehat{y}}\).

Alternatively, using vection notation, we can write \(y = \widehat{y} + e\). To obtain sums, we pre-multiply by \(\imath'\) to obtain \[ \imath'y = \imath'\widehat{y} + \imath'e. \] Since \(\imath'e = 0\) by the first-order condition of least squares, we obtain \(\frac{1}{n}\imath'y = \frac{1}{n}\imath'\widehat{y}\).

Exercise 3

In this exercise, we are going to investigate in more detail under what conditions \(SST = SSR + SSE\) holds.

  1. Show that \[ SST = SSR + SSE + 2\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i})(\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y}). \] Hint: Use telescoping.

  2. Evaluate for the following three models whether the equation reduces to \(SST = SSR + SSE\).

  • The simple linear regression model.
  • The intercept-only model.
  • The no-intercept model (with only one regressor).
  1. Show that we can obtain the decomposition \(y'y = \widehat{y}'\widehat{y} + e'e\) without assuming a constant term, whereas \(SST = SSE + SSR\) can only derived when we do assume a constant term.
CautionHint

  1. Write \(y_{i} - \overline{y}\) as \(y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i} + \widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y}\). Continue with writing out the quadratic term. This should yield the desired result.

  2. Do we obtain \(SST = SSR + SSE\) for the three models?

  • Yes, because we have both first-order conditions.
  • Yes, one of the first-order conditions suffices.
  • No, generally not. One first-order condition is not enough.
  1. Both results are in Section 1.8 of ITE. Note that least squares always gives \(\widehat{y}'e = 0\) (because \(X'e = 0\)), which is needed for the first decomposition. For the second one we need \(\imath'e = 0\) explicitly.

Exercise 4

There is something interesting about the way that we define SSE, the explained sum of squares. In textbooks, you will often read \[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2}. \] However, this definition is different from typical measures of variation. For example, TSS is defined as the sum of squared deviation from its own mean, i.e. \[ SST = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \overline{y})^{2}. \] Let us study \(SSE\) a bit deeper.

  1. Show that, in case of a constant term, \[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2} = \widehat{y}'A\widehat{y}. \]
  2. What changes in a. whenever there is no constant term in the model?
  3. In light of what we found out in this exercise, reconsider exercise 3b for the no-intercept model. Do you think the textbook measure of \(SSE\) is informative in this case?
CautionSolution

  1. We can write \[ \widehat{y}'A\widehat{y} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{\widehat{y}})^{2}, \] by definition. However, with constant term, it is important to see that \(\imath'\widehat{y} = \imath'(y - e) = \imath'y - \imath'e = \imath'y\) because \(\imath'e = 0\) by the first-order condition of least squares.

  2. As shown in a. and in exercise 2 of this week, we no longer have \(\overline{y} = \overline{\widehat{y}}\) in case there is no constant term in the model.

  3. Typically, measures of variation consider the variation around their own mean. In case of a constant term in the model, the definition \[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2}, \] is perfectly accurate because \(\overline{y} = \overline{\widehat{y}}\) due to the first-order condition \(\imath'e = 0\). However, whenever there is no constant term, one could argue that \[ SSE^{*} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{\widehat{y}})^{2}, \] is a much more sensible measure of variation around the fitted values. In light of exercise 3b, this means the following. We used telescoping to show \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i} + \widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2} &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i})^{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2} \\ &\quad \ +2\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \widehat{y}_{i})(\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y}), \end{aligned} \] which we denoted as \(SSR + SSE + \text{additional term}\). However, note that for the no-intercept model, the definition \(SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\widehat{y}_{i} - \overline{y})^{2}\) is arguably not appropriate. Thus, the decomposition does not only fail in terms of the additional term that does not disappear, but the measure of \(SSE\) could be considered questionable/inappropriate as a measure of explained variation.

Exercise 5

Show that the OLS estimator for \(\beta_{2}\) in the simple linear regression model can also be written as \[ \widehat{\beta}_{2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_{i} - \overline{y})x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}} \] Hint: You may start from \(\widehat{\beta}_{2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}}\).

CautionHint

The solution follows directly from writing out the numerator.

Exercise 6

Suppose you demean the independent and dependent variable. Then you estimate a no-intercept model on this data, which we denote (\(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}\)). Show that the estimated slope coefficient for this model is the same as the estimated slope in a simple linear regression model on the original data (not demeaned).

CautionSolution

If we estimate a no-intercept model, the estimator for the slope coefficient is given by \[ \widehat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{x}_{i}\tilde{y_{i}}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tilde{x}_{i}^{2}}. \] Since the variables are demeaned, we have \(\tilde{x}_{i} = x_{i} - \overline{x}\) and \(\tilde{y}_{i} = y_{i} - \overline{y}\). Plugging these expressions in the formula for \(\widehat{\beta}_{2}\) yields the (by now, well-known) estimator \[ \widehat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}}. \]

Exercise 7

Student A estimates the simple linear regression model \[ y_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_i + u_i, \qquad (i=1,\ldots,n), \] by least squares and finds the estimators \(\widehat{\beta}_{1}\) and \(\widehat{\beta}_{2}\). Student B instead estimates the linear regression model \[ y_i = \delta_1 + \delta_2 z_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad (i=1,\ldots,n), \] by least squares, where \(z_{i} = c x_{i}\) for \(c > 0\), i.e. \(z_{i}\) is a rescaled version of \(x_{i}\). She denotes the estimators \(\widehat{\delta}_{1}\) and \(\widehat{\delta}_{2}\).

  1. Show that \(\widehat{\delta}_{2} = c^{-1} \widehat{\beta}_{2}\) and \(\widehat{\delta}_{1} = \widehat{\beta}_{1}\).
  2. How would the results change when \(c < 0\)?
  3. How do the SER and \(R^2\) of both models relate?
CautionSolution

  1. Note that \[ \begin{aligned} \hat{\delta}_{2} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (z_{i} - \overline{z})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (z_{i} - \overline{z})^{2}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (c x_{i} - c \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (c x_{i} - c \overline{x})^{2}} \\ &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (c(x_{i} - \overline{x}))(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (c(x_{i} - \overline{x}))^{2}} = \frac{1}{c} \times \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})(y_{i} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_{i} - \overline{x})^{2}} \\ &= c^{-1} \widehat{\beta}_{2}. \\ \widehat{\delta_{1}} &= \overline{y} - \widehat{\delta}_{2}\overline{z} = \overline{y} - c^{-1} \widehat{\beta}_{2} \times c \overline{x} = \overline{y} - \widehat{\beta}_{2}\overline{x} = \widehat{\beta}_{1}. \end{aligned} \]

  2. From the results in a., we can see that a negative value of \(c\) results in a new slope that has opposite slope. The intercept is not affected.

  3. The SER could only be different if both models generate different fitted values. However, note that \[ \widehat{y}_{i}^{(2)} = \widehat{\delta}_{1} + \widehat{\delta}_{2}z_{i} = \widehat{\beta}_{1} + c^{-1} \widehat{\beta}_{2} \times c x_{i} = \widehat{\beta}_{1} + \widehat{\beta}_{2}x_{i} = \widehat{y}_{i}^{(1)}. \] If the models generate the same fitted values, they also generate the same residuals. Thus since the SSR and SST is the same for both models, their \(R^{2}\) values should also be the same.

Exercise 8

During the course, we often consider the situation of correct specification, i.e. the data-generation process and the model coincide. Let us consider a situation in which this is not true. Suppose the data-generation process is given by \[ y_{i} = \beta_{1} + \beta_{2} x_{i} + u_{i}, \] whereas the model that we estimate is the no-intercept model \[ y_{i} = \beta_{2} x_{i} + u_{i}. \] Is the least-squares estimator of \(\beta_{2}\) in our model unbiased for the true value of \(\beta_{2}\) in the data-generation process?

CautionSolution

The estimator of the slope coefficient based on the no-intercept model is given by \[ \widehat{\beta}_{2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}y_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}}. \] However, the data is generated according to \(y_{i} = \beta_{1} + \beta_{2} x_{i} + u_{i}\). Plugging this into the estimator yields \[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{\beta}_{2} &= \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}(\beta_{1} + \beta_{2} x_{i} + u_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} \\ &= \beta_{1} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} + \beta_{2} + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}u_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}}. \end{aligned} \] We proceed to take expectations: \[ \begin{aligned} E(\widehat{\beta}_{2}) &= E \left(\beta_{1} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} + \beta_{2} + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}u_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} \right) \\ &= \beta_{2} + \beta_{1} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}E(u_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}} \\ &= \beta_{2} + \beta_{1} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}}, \end{aligned} \] where we used that \(x_{i}\) is nonrandom by Assumption 2.2 and \(E(u_{i}) = 0\) by Assumption 2.3. Thus, \(\widehat{\beta}_{2}\) is biased and the bias equals \(\beta_{1} \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_{i}^{2}}\)

Inference and power

Exercise 9

(3.1 in ME) Consider the regression model \(y_{i} = \beta_{1} + \beta_{2}x_{2,i} + \beta_{3}x_{3,i} u_{i}\), where we have data based on \(n=10\) observations on \(y\), \(x_{2}\), and \(x_{3}\), as follows: \[ \begin{aligned} &\imath'y = 20, &\imath'x_{2} = 30, \qquad &\imath'x_{3} = 40 \\ &y'y = 88.2, &x_{2}'x_{2} = 92, \qquad &x_{3}'x_{3} = 163 \\ &x_{2}'y = 59, &x_{3}'y = 88, \qquad &x_{2}'x_{3} = 119. \end{aligned} \] Estimate the three coefficients by least squares and test the null hypothesis that \(\beta_{3}\) equals zero against a two-sided alternative at both the \(5\%\) and \(1\%\) significance level.

Hint: Use a programming language of choice to determine the inverse of \(X'X\).

CautionShort solution

The full solution can be found in the book Mastering Econometrics, Exercise 3.1.

You should find \[ \widehat{\beta} = \begin{pmatrix} -13 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad s^{2} = 3.6, \quad s^{2}v_{33} = 1.44, \quad t = 2.5. \] We reject the null hypothesis at the \(5\%\) significance level, but not at the \(1\%\) significance level.

  3. Increases/Decreases with the distance \(|\theta_{0} - \theta_{1}|\) where \(\theta_{1} \neq \theta_{0}\) denotes the value under the alternative hypothesis \(H_{1}: \theta = \theta_{1}\).
